The GMV equation

Every ecommerce top line - however big the company - is three numbers multiplied together: how many people came, what share of them bought, and how much a purchase was worth. You will never be asked to compute anything in this track. You will be asked to do something more useful: read any store's revenue as three levers, know which team owns each one, and know exactly what to ask when a lever moves.

Part 1 · one equation, whole course

GMV = Traffic x Conversion x AOV 12 min live

This course runs on one fictional store, Mango Lane - a mobile-first fashion app - so every number holds still and cross-checks. In a typical month it gets 360,000 sessions (visits), 2.7% of those sessions end in an order, and the average order is worth $68. Multiply the three and you get the month's top line: $660,960 of GMV (gross merchandise value - the total value of goods sold, before anyone argues about returns and discounts; a2 handles the arguing). Three numbers in, one board number out. There is no fourth lever hiding anywhere.

LiveWhy leaders love this equation (and finance quietly does too)4 min▶

Multiplying a few operational drivers into one headline number is an old, respectable trick: DuPont was decomposing return on equity into margin x turnover x leverage a century ago, and every modern growth model is the same move applied to revenue. The power is not the arithmetic - it is that each factor lands on a different team's desk:

  • Traffic (sessions) - how many visits happened. Bought, earned, or emailed into existence by marketing.
  • Conversion rate - what share of visits ended in an order. Won or lost in the shopping experience, so it belongs to product and UX.
  • AOV - what an average order was worth. Shaped by pricing, bundling, and assortment: merchandising.

When GMV moves, exactly one question matters first: which of the three moved? Everything else in this track builds on that reflex.

Self-studyThe same equation works at any altitude3 min read▶

Traffic x conversion x AOV is not just a whole-company view - it holds for any slice you point it at. Each of Mango Lane's four channels has its own three levers and its own mini-GMV:

  • Organic - $264,384 (40% of GMV)
  • Paid - $198,288 (30%)
  • CRM (email & push) - $132,192 (20%)
  • Social - $66,096 (10%)

The same is true per category, per country, per campaign. When a leader asks "why is paid underperforming?", the answer is always the same three-lever read, one altitude down. The analyst track's b6 (segment cuts) computes these slices; you just need to know they exist and sum to the total.

What to ask your data team after Part 1 "Show me last month's GMV as the three factors - sessions, conversion rate, AOV - side by side with the month before." One slide, six numbers. If they cannot produce it in a day, that is itself a finding (and a6 is for you).
Part 2 · the mental model

You cannot "do GMV" - you can only pull levers 10 min live

GMV is an outcome. No team can walk into the office and work on GMV directly, any more than a football team can work on "the score". Teams can only pull levers - run a campaign (traffic), fix a clunky checkout (conversion), bundle products (AOV) - and the outcome follows. A target set on the outcome without naming a lever is a wish. This distinction sounds obvious and is violated in roughly every second board deck.

LiveThe pass-through idea in plain words4 min▶

Because the three levers are multiplied, a change in any one of them passes straight through to the top line. If traffic falls 10% and nothing else changes, GMV falls about 10%. If AOV rises 10%, GMV rises about 10%. There is no cushion and no delay - the equation has no shock absorber.

Two useful consequences for a leader:

  • Small lever moves are never small. A "minor" 0.3-point conversion dip at Mango Lane (2.7% to 2.4%) is about an 11% haircut on the lever - and therefore about an 11% haircut on GMV. Roughly $73,000 gone in a month, from a change that looks like rounding.
  • Lever moves combine. Traffic up 10% while conversion is down 10% roughly cancels out - the top line looks flat while two big stories hide underneath. Flat GMV is not automatically good news; a3 teaches you to look underneath.
Self-studyWhy each lever needs an owner BEFORE it needs a target4 min read▶

The natural instinct is to set targets first: "conversion to 3% by Q3." But a target on an unowned lever produces a predictable failure mode - when it slips, the review meeting becomes an archaeology dig into whose fault it was, and every team has a plausible alibi (marketing blames site speed, product blames traffic quality, both are sometimes right).

Ownership first reverses the dynamic:

  • An owner watches the lever weekly - so a dip is caught in days, not discovered at quarter end.
  • An owner can explain a move - "conversion dipped because the new checkout shipped Tuesday" is only available from someone who owns the number and the roadmap behind it.
  • Only then does a target mean anything - a target is a promise, and promises need a person attached.

Your homework today is exactly this: levers and owners, no targets yet. Targets come in a5 when you can see benchmarks.

What to ask your data team after Part 2 "For each of the three levers, who is the single named owner - a person, not a department?" If the answer contains the word "shared", ask again.
Demo · read-along

Three moves, three phone calls ★ 15 min · everyone reads

No math here - just reading. Three things happen at Mango Lane. For each one: which lever moved, which team you call, and what you ask. This is the whole leadership skill: right lever, right owner, right question, in that order.

LiveScenario 1 - sessions dip 10% after an ad budget cut5 min▶

Finance trimmed paid spend mid-month. Two weeks later the weekly report shows sessions down about 10%, and GMV down about the same. Nobody should be surprised - a traffic lever move passes straight through - but somebody should own the response.

Which lever: traffic. Conversion and AOV are steady, so the top line fell purely because fewer visits happened.

Who you call: marketing - traffic is their lever, and the budget cut hit their channel mix.

What you ask: "Which channel lost the sessions, and were they sessions that used to convert?" Cheap traffic converting at 0.5% and CRM traffic converting at 5% are not the same loss - the answer decides whether the cut was smart or expensive.

LiveScenario 2 - conversion dips after a checkout redesign5 min▶

Product shipped a sleeker checkout on the 1st. By the 10th, conversion has drifted from 2.7% toward 2.4%. Traffic is normal, AOV is normal - GMV is quietly bleeding through one lever, and the timing is not a coincidence until proven otherwise.

Which lever: conversion. Same number of visits, same order size - a smaller share of visits is finishing the purchase.

Who you call: product and UX - the shopping experience is their lever, and they just changed it.

What you ask: "Where in the journey are shoppers now dropping, compared to before the release - and can we roll back while we investigate?" You do not need the funnel vocabulary yet (a3 gives it to you); you need the date of the change and a before/after picture.

LiveScenario 3 - AOV rises after bundling5 min▶

Merchandising launched "complete the look" bundles. AOV climbs from $68 toward $75, and GMV rises with it. Good news - but good news deserves the same discipline as bad news, because you want to know whether it will still be there next quarter.

Which lever: AOV. Same visits, same share buying - each order simply got bigger.

Who you call: merchandising - to congratulate them, and then to interrogate the win.

What you ask: "Is the lift from more items per order or pricier items - and did conversion hold while AOV rose?" Bundles sometimes lift order size while scaring off smaller buyers; a real win moves one lever without quietly denting another.

Self-studyWhen two levers move at once3 min read▶

Real months are rarely as clean as the three scenarios above. A sale event typically pushes traffic and conversion up while pulling AOV down; an ad cut pulls traffic down while conversion drifts up (the remaining visitors are the keen ones). Two habits keep multi-lever months readable:

  • Ask for all three levers every time, even when the answer seems obvious. The lever nobody mentioned is where surprises hide.
  • Ask which lever moved because of us and which moved on its own. A deliberate trade (discount lowers AOV, lifts conversion) is strategy; the same picture arriving uninvited is a problem.

a3 gives you the funnel view that makes multi-lever months quick to read, and a4 puts the reading on a weekly rhythm so no lever drifts unwatched for long.

Real world

The pattern to keep: in all three scenarios the first question was never "why is GMV down/up?" - it was "which lever moved?" GMV itself never gets a phone call. Levers have owners; outcomes only have meetings.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 15-20 min total

  • Write your own business's top line as the three levers: traffic x conversion x average order (or your industry's equivalents - visits, win rate, deal size work the same way). Rough numbers are fine; the structure is the exercise.
  • Next to each lever, write one named owner - a person, not a team. Note where you had to hesitate: hesitation is the finding.
  • Ask your data team the Part 1 question: last month's three factors next to the month before, on one slide.
  • Bring your three-levers-with-owners sheet to a2 - the metric dictionary will sharpen every word on it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Which of these is an outcome rather than a lever?

GMV is the result of the three levers multiplied - no team can work on it directly. Sessions, conversion rate, and AOV are the levers teams actually pull.

2 · Traffic falls 10% while conversion rate and AOV stay flat. What happens to GMV?

The levers are multiplied, so a 10% drop in one factor (with the others flat) is about a 10% drop in the product. No cushion, no amplification - straight pass-through.

3 · Conversion rate dips the week after a site release. Which team gets the first call?

Right lever, right owner: conversion belongs to product and UX, and a dip timed to a release points at the release. More traffic would paper over a leak instead of fixing it.
