Part 1 · covers IRP Commerce, Dynamic Yield, Littledata

What good looks like 15 min live

"Average ecommerce conversion rate" is a nearly useless number because verticals differ by a factor of ten. IRP Commerce's June 2026 panel puts the overall session CR at 2.03% - but look at the spread underneath it. A craft-supplies store at 3% is underperforming; a baby-goods store at 1% is a star.

LiveThree panels, three flavors of "good"5 min▶

Three widely quoted benchmark sources, each seeing a different slice of the world:

  • IRP Commerce (June 2026) - overall 2.03% (up from 1.85% a year earlier); the vertical table above. Its base is IRP's own merchant panel.
  • Dynamic Yield (rolling, 200M+ unique shoppers) - the extremes: beauty converts at 5.37% over 12 months while luxury & jewelry sits at 0.71%. Same internet, 7x apart - vertical is destiny.
  • Littledata Shopify benchmark, 2025 - average Shopify store CR 1.4%, and you need above 4.7% to be in the top 10%. Down-funnel: average checkout completion is 45%, top 10% reach 66%.
Honesty note about our running store Mango Lane (2.7% session CR, 60% checkout completion) is a deliberately healthy fashion store - above IRP's fashion 1.70% and well above Littledata's 45% average completion. It was built that way so the course math stays clean. Do not read it as "typical"; read it as "achievable".
Self-studyWhy the bars differ so much by vertical3 min read▶

The spread is not noise - it is purchase behavior. Craft supplies and pet food are repeat, low-consideration buys: the shopper knows exactly what she wants and reorders. Fashion involves browsing, fit anxiety, and comparison across stores. Luxury involves weeks of consideration for one purchase. Baby & child skews to heavy research across many sessions before one big cart.

  • High CR verticals - repeat purchases, known SKUs, low ticket. Conversion is the default outcome of a visit.
  • Low CR verticals - high consideration, high ticket, long journeys. Most sessions are legitimate research, not failures.

Consequence for you as a leader: a target imported from another vertical is not ambitious, it is innumerate.

Part 2 · panels and denominators

Benchmark hygiene: whose number is that? 8 min live

Every benchmark is an average over somebody's stores. IRP averages its own merchant base. Dynamic Yield sees large enterprise brands. Littledata sees Shopify stores - which skew smaller. That is why "average CR" can be 2.03% in one report and 1.4% in another and both are right. And underneath the panel question sits the a2 question you already know: is the denominator sessions or visitors?

LiveNever mix panels in one comparison5 min▶

The classic broken slide: "We are at 2.7% (Shopify, sessions), industry is at 3.5% (some enterprise panel, possibly visitors) - we lag." Three ways that comparison can be fiction:

  • Different store base - enterprise brands vs Shopify long tail are different planets. Compare against the panel that looks like you.
  • Different denominator - session-based and visitor-based CR differ by ~40% at Mango Lane (2.7% vs ~3.9%) with zero business difference. Mixing them makes gaps appear and vanish at will.
  • Different period - a November benchmark vs your March number bakes seasonality into the "gap".
The best benchmark is in your own warehouse Your store, same month last year, same definitions, same tracking. It controls for vertical, panel, and denominator all at once. External benchmarks set the ambition level; your own last year sets the trend line. Trust the second more.
Self-studyThe two questions before trusting any benchmark2 min read▶

Before any external number is allowed into your review, someone answers these out loud:

  • 1 · Whose stores? What panel produced this - enterprise brands, Shopify long tail, one platform's merchants, one country? Does that base resemble us?
  • 2 · What denominator? Sessions or visitors (or orders, for value metrics)? Does it match how we compute the same metric?

If nobody in the room can answer both, the benchmark is decoration, not evidence. Park it and move on - a meeting that argues against an unverified bar is a meeting wasted twice.

Part 3 · the calendar is a confounder

Seasonality: compare like with like 9 min live

Fashion breathes with the calendar: holiday gifting, season changeovers (spring and autumn drops), and sale events each pull demand forward or push it back. So a raw week-over-week move mixes two stories - what the business did and what the calendar did. The cure is choosing the right comparison window, not staring harder at the chart.

LiveThe one rule: YoY at peaks, WoW/MoM in BAU5 min▶

Keep two baselines and know which one you are standing on:

  • Business-as-usual (BAU) weeks - no campaign, no season change. Here WoW and MoM are meaningful: the calendar is roughly constant, so a move is probably the business.
  • Peak or campaign weeks - holiday, a sale event, a season drop. Here WoW is meaningless (of course sale week beats the week before it). Compare against the same peak last year: this Black-Friday week vs last Black-Friday week.
Real world

The post-sale "collapse". The week after a big sale, GMV drops hard and someone calls an emergency meeting. But a sale pulls demand forward - shoppers who would have bought next week bought this week at a discount. The drop after is usually gravity, not disease. The tell: compare the post-sale week to the post-sale week last year. If it is in line, nothing is wrong.

Self-studyFashion's calendar in one list2 min read▶

The peaks a fashion store like Mango Lane lives around - worth writing into the review calendar so nobody is surprised twice a year:

  • Holiday gifting - the November-December wave; the biggest YoY-only zone of the year.
  • Season changeovers - spring and autumn collection drops lift traffic and PDP views; markdowns on outgoing stock lift CR but compress AOV.
  • Sale events - planned promotions pull demand forward. Expect the trough after; budget for it in the forecast so it never reads as a surprise.

Three judgment calls ★ 8 min · no computation

Three moments that actually happen in reviews. For each: the reflex a benchmark-literate leader applies before reacting.

LiveCall it: bad number or bad comparison?8 min▶

1 · "Our CR is 1.9% - that is terrible." Terrible against what? This is a fashion store: IRP's fashion bar is 1.70%, so 1.9% is above vertical peers. Against the all-vertical 2.03% it looks weak - but that average is mostly other people's categories. Verdict: context first, judgment second.

2 · "GMV is down 30% in the first week of January - panic?" The first week of January follows the biggest peak of the year. That is post-peak gravity. The check: same week last year. If January week 1 is down 30% vs December every year, the business is behaving normally. Verdict: YoY before alarm bells.

3 · "Industry average is 2.03%, so our target is 2.03%." Wrong panel (IRP's merchant base, not yours), wrong vertical (that average is dominated by non-fashion categories), and unstated denominator. A fashion store beating 1.70% is doing fine; importing 2.03% as a target punishes the team for the category they work in. Verdict: targets come from your vertical's bar and your own last year.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 15 min total

  • Pull your vertical's row from any benchmark source you trust (IRP, Dynamic Yield, Littledata, or your platform's own report). Write the number down.
  • Next to it, write the two hygiene answers before you ever quote it: whose stores is this panel, and what denominator does it use?
  • Mark your business's two or three peaks on a 12-month calendar. For each, note the comparison you will accept in reviews: YoY at the peak, WoW/MoM elsewhere.
  • Optional: find last year's post-peak trough in your numbers. Knowing its usual depth is the cheapest panic-prevention you will ever buy.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A fashion store reports 1.9% session CR. Your best first read?

Vertical first. The 2.03% overall average is dominated by other categories; fashion's bar is 1.70%, so 1.9% is above peers. Comparisons are fine - they just have to be against the right panel and vertical.

2 · GMV falls 30% in the week after your biggest sale of the year. What is the right check before reacting?

A sale pulls next week's demand into sale week, so the trough after is gravity, not disease. During and around peaks the honest comparison is YoY; WoW only works in business-as-usual weeks.

3 · Before any external benchmark is allowed into your review, which two questions must be answered?

Panel and denominator are the two silent assumptions that make benchmark gaps appear or vanish. Recency and sample size matter too - but a fresh, huge panel of stores unlike yours, measured on a different denominator, is still fiction.
